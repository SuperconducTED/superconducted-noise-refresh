# Related work draft: stale calibrations and simulator noise models

Status: draft for the related-work section, not yet verified by the author. Statements from eleven of its sources have since been checked against the texts: Müller et al. (Fig. 7, device, duration), Vishwakarma (devices, Aer models, medians, the stale mapping), the vendor guide (cadence), Huo et al. (no significant relation up to 24 hours; the 72-hour figure), Kurniawan et al. (latest against averaged calibration; two backends and a simulator; its conclusion), Wilson et al. (measurement just before execution), both Dasgupta and Humble papers (the 22-month metrics; the monthly distributions over 16 months), Li et al. (abstract), Hu et al. (one year, model repository) and Kourousias et al. (the minimum-cost field set only). No row checked so far was found wrong. Everything else rests on one reading, and every row needs the author's own reading before it is cited. Three sentences written elsewhere from summaries of these papers were wrong when checked; `docs/lessons-learned.md` lists them. Section 4 lists what could not be verified. Literature as reachable on 2026-11-13. Every factual statement about a paper carries a pointer (section, figure, table or appendix) into that paper. "Inferred" marks a reading that the paper does not state outright. Q1 to Q5 and "side result" refer to the study's own questions:

- Q1: how calibration changes arrive (a few components, or whole families in batches).
- Q2: how fast the simulated output of a noise model built from an older calibration departs from one built from the current calibration, against calibration age.
- Q3: which calibration family (T1/T2, readout, gate error) carries that departure, by refreshing one family at a time, and its circuit dependence.
- Q4: a refresh rule (cheapest set of families within a stated distance), and forecasting against change detection.
- Q5: whether any of it holds against hardware.
- Side result: building the noise model only for the circuit's qubits.

## 1. Closest work first

### 1.1 Verdict

No paper read here does the whole study, and none measures the distance between the outputs of two calibration-derived noise models as a function of the age of one of them. Four published works and one unread repository overlap enough that the article must position itself against them explicitly.

1. **Müller et al. 2025 (arXiv:2504.08313)** is the largest single overlap. Its Fig. 7 plots the mean total variation distance between hardware and the calibration-driven model "over time since the last calibration" across a 33-hour data collection, with one curve per error channel switched off (Sec. V; Fig. 7 caption). That one figure combines an age axis (Q2), a per-channel attribution (Q3) and a hardware reference (Q5). In the plotted data the full model's distance stays between roughly 0.14 and 0.165 over about 34 hours with no steady growth, and the curves for the switched-off channels move in parallel with it; removing state preparation and measurement error raises the distance most (to roughly 0.26 to 0.29), removing two-qubit gate error next (roughly 0.19 to 0.21), while the idle and always-on channels are barely distinguishable from the full model (Fig. 7, values read from the plot by eye). It differs from the study in every other respect: a five-qubit flux-tunable device that is not an IBM machine (Sec. IV), one calibration and one continuous window (Sec. V; Fig. 7 axis), a model with fitted parameters (Sec. V, Table I), channels switched off rather than one calibration family refreshed, and no model-against-model distance.
2. **Vishwakarma 2026 (Quantum Sci. Technol. 11, 0450a5)**, published 30 October 2026, works on the same processor family and partly the same device (ibm_fez, ibm_marrakesh, ibm_kingston; Sec. 2.3), builds Qiskit Aer noise models from archived backend-property snapshots on nine dates (Sec. 2.3, Table 1), compares them with hardware (Sec. 3.3, 3.4), repeats identical circuits 9.3 to 12.1 days later and reports the hardware-to-hardware total variation distance (Sec. 3.5), and tests a stale mapping against a refreshed one with a gate-only, gate-plus-readout and full-calibration breakdown (Sec. 3.6, Fig. 5). Its object is the compiler's mapping decision, not the noise model's output, and it states that calibration age was recorded but not identified as a cause (Sec. 2.3, Sec. 4.7).
3. **Kourousias et al. 2026 (arXiv:2610.09931)** poses a formal version of Q4 for provenance records: the minimum-cost set of fields such that the worst-case Hellinger distance between output distributions sharing a record stays under a tolerance (Definition 1, Eq. 2; Proposition 2). It also argues that one-field-at-a-time omission checks do not compose (Sec. I example; Sec. IV; Sec. V), which is a direct methodological challenge to a refresh-one-family-at-a-time design (Q3) and to any rule built from single-family results (Q4). Its fields are synthetic grid levels, with no calibration time series and no T1/T2 (Sec. V).
4. **Hu et al. 2023 (arXiv:2304.04666)** replays a year of daily calibration records of one five-qubit IBM device as simulator noise models and follows a circuit tuned to the first day's calibration through the later days (Sec. 2.2, Fig. 2; Sec. 4.1). It then proposes a reuse-or-refresh rule: keep a repository of tuned circuits and re-optimise only when the current calibration is farther than a threshold from every stored one, in a distance weighted by each calibration entry's correlation with performance (Sec. 3.3, Sec. 3.4). This overlaps Q2 by analogy and Q4 in part. The quantity that ages is a trained circuit and the measure is task accuracy; the noise model itself is always current.
5. **An unread course-project repository** described in the prior novelty check (its Sec. 3.1) as "staleness-aware noise modeling": Aer simulation from published calibration numbers with queue delays of 1 to 48 hours, merged distinct calibration states, 120 days on three machines, no hardware. If that description is accurate it overlaps Q2 over the 1 to 48 hour range. It was not opened and nothing about it is verified here (see Sec. 4).

### 1.2 By question

**Q1 (how changes arrive). Open in the papers read; partly described by vendor documentation.** No paper analyses whether published calibration values change a few at a time or by family. The vendor guide "Monitor, calibrate, and benchmark" documents the mechanism without statistics: parameter monitoring about once an hour; calibrations triggered when monitoring flags a deviation, run only on the flagged qubits or gates plus those required by batching rules; separate single-qubit, two-qubit and readout calibration groups; and the published properties coming from daily benchmarking with a timestamp per property, while `last_update_date` moves whenever any calibration runs (sections "Monitor parameters", "Calibrate", "Benchmark"). From this description one would expect family-wise batches and flagged subsets (inferred; the guide gives no statistics). It also says the published properties derive from daily benchmarking; the study's premise that values are re-measured many times a day needs to be reconciled with this using its own Q1 data. Descriptive fragments elsewhere: a custom schedule with a full recalibration in the morning and a two-qubit-only one in the evening (Yeter-Aydeniz et al., Sec. 3, App. A); per-field calibration timestamps in the archived records (Dasgupta and Humble 2021, App. A-A); a compilation method that treats qubits not calibrated on the day separately (Kurniawan et al., Sec. III-C, Table II); database entries from recalibrations outside the daily routine (Carroll et al., App. J); an hourly 39-day error series naming the most variable components (Ji, Brandhofer and Polian, Sec. I, Fig. 2); and stationarity tests in which CNOT error is the least stationary family (Baheri et al., Sec. IV, Fig. 6).

**Q2 (departure against age). Not answered for model against model.** The age axis exists in the literature only against hardware or against the ideal distribution:

- hardware against model, 0 to about 34 hours since calibration, five-qubit device, with no steady growth in the plotted distance (Müller et al., Fig. 7, read from the plot); the paper concludes the model still predicts after more than 30 hours (Sec. VII);
- hardware output error against time since the daily calibration, up to 24 hours, with no significant relationship, and stable error for one algorithm over 72 hours (Huo et al., Sec. IV-B, RQ5 and RQ6, Fig. 7, Fig. 8, Table V);
- the opposite reading: compiling with only the latest calibration produced a drop of more than 30% on one day on hardware, while the same compilation looked best in a simulator loaded with that same calibration (Kurniawan et al., Sec. IV-B, Fig. 6);
- hardware against hardware for identical circuits after 9.3 to 12.1 days, median total variation distance 0.146 and 0.154 (Vishwakarma, Sec. 3.5);
- one stale snapshot model against current models, one test, age not stated: 75.6 to 78.5% similarity against 95.1 to 98.1% among the current ones (Bautra et al., Sec. 4.1, Table 2);
- simulated stability of one observable under monthly noise distributions over 16 months, relative to the first month (Dasgupta and Humble 2024, Sec. 3.3, Fig. 8);
- noise models derived from ibm_fez at successive times, used to compare two layout-selection methods (Srivastava et al., Sec. 6.2, Fig. 12);
- stale against re-estimated noise parameters at 13 time points within one day on hardware (Dasgupta and Humble 2024, PEC paper, Sec. V);
- simulated accuracy of a circuit tuned to day one under each later day's calibration-derived model, over a year of daily records (Hu et al., Sec. 2.2, Fig. 2).

None gives a curve of model-to-model distance from hours to 30 days from an hourly archive. The cadences these papers assume differ (Sec. 1.3), so their staleness axes are not directly comparable with an hourly one.

**Q3 (which family). The static ranking is well covered; the staleness ranking is not.** Several papers switch a noise channel or a calibration feature on and off and score the result, mostly against hardware: Müller et al. (Fig. 7; Sec. VI: state preparation and measurement and gate errors dominate, idle and crosstalk channels matter little); Maschek et al. (Sec. IV-B, Fig. 6: readout and gate error dominate for three-qubit GHZ, decoherence matters for idle circuits and is then suspected for deep QAOA, Sec. IV-C); Georgopoulos et al. (Table 3: relaxation alone explains little); Bravo-Montes et al. (Sec. 4.1.1, Tables 4 and 5); Weber et al. (Sec. 4.4; Sec. 5, Fig. 5 and 6: readout-only against the full device model); Dahlhauser and Humble (Sec. IV, Fig. 8); Aseguinolaza et al. (Fig. 5: elapsed time, that is T1/T2, is the largest term for their circuits, the opposite ranking); Al Farib et al. (Table VIII: two-qubit error, then readout, then T1/T2, by blanking inputs of a learned mitigator); Li et al. (Fig. 11: T1/T2-only against all fields as predictor inputs); Vishwakarma (Sec. 3.6: gate error carries the selector gain, readout and duration terms add about nothing). All of these ask which family matters to the output or to a decision at one moment. None asks which family's change over time moves the output, and none refreshes one family while holding the others stale. The article should state this distinction, because a reviewer will know the static result. Kourousias et al. give a reason to distrust conclusions from single-family experiments when families interact (Sec. IV, Sec. V).

**Q4 (refresh rule; forecast against detection). No refresh rule for a simulator noise model was found.** Structural neighbours: a minimum-cost field set under a distance tolerance (Kourousias et al., Eq. 2); a cheap probe that triggers full characterisation only on detected change, with an explicit cost model, which names detection of a stale learned noise model as future work (Chen et al., Sec. III-A, Sec. VII); a when-to-recalibrate controller on a phenomenological ageing model (Deng, Sec. II to IV); re-running only a cheap mapping step when error rates have changed significantly, with no threshold given (Ji, Brandhofer and Polian, Sec. III); a staleness gate that re-reads the snapshot before submission (Bansal et al., Sec. IV-E); a threshold on a performance-weighted distance between calibration records that decides whether a stored circuit is reused or re-optimised (Hu et al., Sec. 3.4); a vendor tutorial that states the cost of refreshing all properties, about 70 seconds of processor time on a 156-qubit device (tutorial, "Scope and limits of the refreshed properties"). On forecasting: calibration values have been forecast with classical time-series models (Baheri et al., Sec. V) and with neural models on an hourly archive of three 127-qubit devices (Dahi et al., Sec. III-B, Sec. IV-B2), and performance bounds have been predicted from 700 days of noise history (Li et al., Sec. VI-A); none of these is compared with a no-change or change-detection baseline for the purpose of refreshing a model. Two statements cut against forecasting: history is said to predict non-stationary noise poorly (Dasgupta and Humble 2024, PEC paper, Sec. III), and daily T1 is autocorrelated over only one to two days and reverts to its mean (Carroll et al., App. B, App. D). The only head-to-head comparison of forecasting with shift detection under a cost ratio that was read is outside quantum computing (Regol et al., Sec. 5 and 6).

**Q5 (hardware). Prior work is a threat to significance rather than a pre-emption.** Several papers find that calibration-derived noise models, most of them of the Aer type, miss hardware by a sizeable margin; that this margin may exceed any staleness effect is inferred here, since none of them makes the comparison. The findings: a slope of 0.28 between emulated and measured compiler effects, sign agreement in 35 of 54 cases, and almost no improvement when the model is rebuilt from execution-time properties (Vishwakarma, Sec. 3.3, Sec. 3.4); simulators agreeing with each other far more than with hardware (Bautra et al., Sec. 4, Tables 2 to 4); a fitted model cutting the Hellinger distance of the default device model by about half (Ji et al., Sec. III.1.2); residual distances of about 0.15 (Müller et al., Sec. VI) and 0.13 ± 0.07 (Maschek et al., Sec. IV-B) for small circuits; reported gate errors understating cycle-benchmarked ones (Yeter-Aydeniz et al., Sec. 4.2); calibration-based fidelity estimates treated as loose upper bounds (Nation and Treinish, Sec. V); part of the gap between reported and freshly measured properties being methodology, not drift (vendor tutorial, "Compare the measured properties against the reported ones"); and error rates that switch in steps lasting about 100 seconds (Hirasaki et al., Fig. 1). If the study's hardware check finds a model-to-hardware distance of this size, the model-to-model staleness distances of Q2 have to be read against it.

**Side result (noise model for the circuit's qubits only). Not found as a studied question.** Adjacent practice only: a ten-qubit subgraph cut from 156-qubit targets for tractability (Tomar and Peddireddy, Sec. III); each small circuit simulated with its own qubits' data (Maschek et al., Sec. IV-B); noise-model construction counted inside emulator runtime (Bravo-Montes et al., Sec. 3.2.4); noise construction time reported as near constant across circuit sizes on a 156-qubit snapshot (Liu et al., Sec. VI-A).

## 3. Per-paper notes (closest fifteen)

**Müller et al. 2025.** The model takes T1, T2, readout and gate fidelities from the device's routine calibration and adds n fitted parameters (Sec. I; Sec. V, Table I). Data were taken for 33 hours after one calibration, in clusters of eight circuits 15 to 30 minutes apart (Sec. V). Fig. 7 shows the hardware-to-model TVD against time since that calibration, for the full model and for each of five channels switched off (Sec. V). The plotted full-model distance wanders between about 0.14 and 0.165 over roughly 34 hours without steady growth, and all curves move together, which suggests the variation comes from the device rather than from any one modelled channel (inferred from the plot). The conclusion drawn is that prediction survives more than 30 hours (Sec. VII). Discussion ranks state preparation and measurement and gate errors above idle and crosstalk channels (Sec. VI). The device is a five-qubit non-IBM processor with four qubits used (Sec. IV), and 12.5% of the data train the fit (Sec. V). This is the closest precedent for presenting Q2, Q3 and Q5 together, and the study's figures will be compared with it.

**Vishwakarma 2026.** The paper separates three uses of calibration data: finding candidate mappings, predicting performance through an Aer model built from the same data, and refreshing a mapping later (Sec. 4.1). The Aer model is the standard backend-properties construction (Sec. 2.4), built from snapshots that were 14.3 to 81.7 hours old at execution (Sec. 2.3). Against hardware it reproduces the sign of the compiler effect in 35 of 54 cases with a slope of 0.28, and a model rebuilt from execution-associated properties does about the same (Sec. 3.3, 3.4). Identical circuits repeated after a median of 11.8 days keep their relative ordering in 16 of 18 cases although their output distributions move by a median TVD of about 0.15 (Sec. 3.5). The author writes that "the design therefore cannot identify a causal calibration-age effect" (Sec. 4.7). For the study this is the nearest competitor on the same devices. That a model rebuilt from execution-time properties does no better suggests that model form, not staleness, may dominate the gap to hardware (inferred from Sec. 3.3 and 3.4; the paper does not put it this way).

**Kurniawan et al. 2024.** Calibration data were collected daily for months on two devices and fed to noise-aware compilers as the latest value, an all-time average, a mix, or an n-day window (Sec. III-C, Table II). On hardware the latest-only variant suffered a drop of more than 30% on one day that the 15-day window avoided (Sec. IV-B, Fig. 6a). In a simulator loaded with the same latest calibration, the latest-only variant looked best, because compiler and model share their input (Sec. III-F, Sec. IV-B). The study focuses on two-qubit and readout error (Sec. IV-B) and states average gains of 0.08% and 8.32% on the two devices (Sec. I). It never builds two models of different ages.

**Huo et al. 2025.** A month of daily hardware runs of 16 algorithms on 127-qubit devices (Sec. III). Output error shows no monotonic trend with hours since the daily calibration (Sec. IV-B, RQ5, Fig. 7, Table V), and one algorithm keeps a stable error over 72 hours on three devices (RQ6, Fig. 8). The authors conclude that frequent re-transpilation may be unnecessary (Sec. VI). The abstract counts five devices while Sec. I and Sec. III list six. The finding conflicts in spirit with Kurniawan et al. and with Wilson et al.; all three are hardware-against-ideal results about compiled circuits, so none settles what happens to a noise model.

**Dasgupta and Humble 2024 (ACM TQC).** Daily characterisation data of one 127-qubit device over 16 months are turned into monthly joint distributions of 16 parameters with a copula (Sec. 3.3.1, 3.3.2, Table 1). A five-qubit circuit is simulated in Qiskit under noise sampled from each month, with channels for dephasing, single-qubit and CNOT depolarising, and readout (Sec. 3.3.4, 3.3.5). The quantity tracked is the change in mean success probability relative to the first month, compared with an analytic bound (Sec. 2.2, Sec. 3.3.6, Fig. 8). The distance reported over time is between parameter distributions, 0.41 to 0.92 after normalisation (Sec. 4, Fig. 6). Fig. 7 apportions that distance among parameters; the text and the caption describe the result differently (Sec. 3.3.3; Fig. 7 caption).

**Bautra et al. 2026.** Four Aer variants are compared with hardware on two 127-qubit devices: a model built from downloadable calibration files, two backend-derived variants, and a fake-backend snapshot whose calibration is not current (Sec. 3.5, Sec. 3.7). Jobs were grouped to limit calibration drift (section "Hardware, Data Collection, and Experimental Matrix"). In the one similarity matrix printed, the three current variants agree with each other at 95.1 to 98.1% and the stale snapshot at 75.6 to 78.5%, and the stale snapshot is furthest from hardware (Sec. 4.1, Table 2). Across 24 tests the file-built model is closest to hardware most often (Table 4). The snapshot's age is not given and the comparison is not repeated over time.

**Srivastava et al. 2026.** The paper argues that layout scores computed from calibration data can be wrong because that data is 3 to 24 hours old (Sec. 1, Sec. 2.2) and proposes a cheap probe circuit run just before execution. Its Sec. 6.2 uses noise models derived from ibm_fez at different time instances (Fig. 12) and synthetic snapshots with stronger variation (Fig. 13). In the real-snapshot case the two layout choices stay close over time; in the synthetic case the probe often wins. Snapshot count and spacing are not stated. This is the nearest use of successive calibration-derived models of the study's own device, applied to a decision rather than to a distance.

**Kourousias et al. 2026.** A record is sufficient when any two executions sharing it have output distributions within a Hellinger tolerance; the cheapest sufficient field set is a weighted hitting-set problem (Definition 1, Eq. 2, Proposition 2). A worked QAOA example shows two fields that each pass an omission check alone and fail together (Sec. I; Sec. V), and Sec. IV argues that averaging-style ablation understates the worst case. Selected minimum sets always retain gate-noise strength and readout error (Table 2). The paper states that it does not certify future calibrations (Sec. VII; Sec. VIII). For the study it supplies both a formal template for Q4 and a warning about inferring a multi-family rule from single-family refreshes.

**Wilson et al. 2020.** The authors measure readout and two-qubit error themselves shortly before execution and compare the resulting layouts with ones from the vendor's daily data (Sec. IV, V). Fresh measurements give better hardware results on most days, with vendor data about 10 hours old in one case and 28 to 49 hours old in another (Sec. V-A, V-B). They caution that the benefit varies by day (Observations 2 and 5) and that hourly readout fidelity drifts irregularly (Sec. II-A, Fig. 1). Their error values differ from the vendor's by method, so the gain is not attributable to age alone (inferred from the authors' own caveats in Sec. V-A, V-D and V-E).

**Maschek et al. 2025.** A channel model built from calibration values is validated against three IBM devices, including ibm_fez for QAOA (Sec. IV). For three-qubit GHZ circuits the hardware-to-ideal distance tracks readout and gate error far more than T1/T2 (Sec. IV-B, Fig. 6), while reported T1 matches idle decay (Sec. IV-A, Fig. 5). Decoherence was therefore dropped for QAOA and then named as a likely cause of over-optimistic predictions on larger circuits (Sec. IV-C). The paper notes hardware results worsening within 12 hours while the simulation stays fixed (Sec. VI). It anticipates the circuit dependence that Q3 will report, on a static basis.

**Dahi et al.** Calibrations of three 127-qubit devices were extracted hourly for 3.5 months (Sec. III-A). The device-wide medians of five qubit properties are forecast with neural models, and a second model maps them to the simulated Jensen-Shannon divergence between noisy and ideal outputs (Sec. III-B, Sec. IV-B). A third experiment simulates circuits under calibrations from several time steps (Sec. IV-B3, Table VI). Gate errors are left out, there is no hardware, and no persistence baseline is reported. This is the closest prior use of an hourly IBM calibration archive with a simulator.

**Li et al. 2025 (QuBound).** The premise is that noise changes faster than repeated noisy simulation can finish, illustrated by T1 profiled every seven minutes (Sec. IV-B, Fig. 4). Training traces come from noisy simulation over 700 days of noise history at four samples a day (Sec. VI-A). The model predicts bounds on outcome probabilities and observable expectations for a fixed circuit (Sec. III-C, III-D; Sec. VII). An ablation shows that encoding only T1 and T2 lowers bound compliance compared with also encoding gate and readout errors (Sec. VI-B4, Fig. 11). It forecasts performance from calibration history but does not compare stale and current models.

**Dasgupta and Humble 2024 (PEC).** Thirteen complete datasets were taken on one device within one day (Sec. V). Readout fidelity and CNOT depolarising strength, inferred from the outputs, moved appreciably within the day (Sec. V-A, Fig. 6). Mitigation with re-estimated parameters beat mitigation with parameters frozen at the first time point by 42% in accuracy on average, with one time point where the frozen values did better (Sec. V-C, Fig. 8). The paper also asserts that history predicts such noise poorly (Sec. III). It is hardware evidence on a 2-hour grid that parameters a few hours old cost accuracy, in a mitigation setting.

**Hu et al. 2023.** A year of daily calibration records of one five-qubit device is replayed as a sequence of simulator noise models (Sec. 4.1). A variational classifier trained against the first day's calibration holds its simulated accuracy for about three weeks and then collapses when error rates rise (Sec. 2.2, Fig. 2), and a circuit that failed on one date works again nine days later (Sec. 2.2, Observation 3, Fig. 4). The proposed manager keeps a repository of circuits, each tied to a representative calibration record, and decides between reuse and re-optimisation by a threshold on a weighted distance between the current record and the stored ones, the weights being the correlation of each calibration entry with performance (Sec. 3.3; Sec. 3.4, Guidance 1). Evaluation covers 146 days in simulation and one hardware device (Sec. 4.2, Table 1). It is the nearest published reuse-or-refresh rule driven by calibration history, but the object refreshed is a trained circuit, the simulator always uses the current day's calibration, and no distance between model outputs is reported.

**Regol et al. 2025.** The retraining decision is cast as minimising a cost that adds retraining cost to performance loss over a horizon (Sec. 3, Eq. 1). The proposed method forecasts the future performance of current and retrained models with uncertainty (Sec. 4) and is compared with three shift-detection triggers and a cost-aware baseline (Sec. 5). It tracks the oracle more closely across cost ratios, while detection triggers do well only when retraining is cheap (Sec. 6). The setting is classification under distribution shift, so it informs the design of Q4's comparison and nothing about quantum noise.
